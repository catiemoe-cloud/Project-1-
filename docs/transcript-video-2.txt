In this video, I’ll be showing how my Apple valuation model works, how someone can run it, and the validations that I completed in the process.

To run the project, a user needs Python 3.9 or later. The analysis script creates visible output so you can see the inputs, valuation date, results, and checks without running the model.

Next, looking at the model itself, it starts with Apple’s fiscal-year 2025 audited balance sheet and forecasts fiscal year 2026 to fiscal year 2030. My base assumptions are 6% revenue growth, 47% gross margin, 15% operating expense, 3% capex, 9% WACC, and 2.5% terminal growth.

The first thing in running our model is running our src Apple FCFF model. When I run this model, the base value is $131.61 per share, and both statement checks are zero for all five years. My low, base, and high cases are $87, $131, and $185.85. The WACC and terminal-growth sensitivity ranges from $109.06 to $167.57. My reverse-DCF check could not reach $337 with constant growth from negative 10% to 30%, keeping other inputs fixed.

Next, running our analysis and our peer valuation, the analysis script creates visible output from the same model, so I do not have to manually type results into the report. For the peer cross-check, I used Microsoft and Alphabet based on the policy created before selecting them. Their P/E results imply $206.86 to $239.69 per share, which I did not average into the DCF because their businesses differ from Apple.

Now, going over to my GitHub to show the documents created. For the enterprise-to-equity bridge, I added $146 billion and $517 million of cash and marketable securities, subtracted $84 billion and $344 million of debt, and divided by 14 billion and 594.18 million shares. The documented WACC build is 9.45%. The model, however, uses a 9% whole-percent base input.

Next, looking at my validation, the biggest risk was that the statements could look correct but not actually articulate. I tested that first, along with the Week 3 training-case answer, filing-based bridge values, WACC inputs, and expected DCF direction.

For my locked changed-input test, I made an AI-off prediction before running the test. I changed only revenue growth from 6% to 9%. The value increased from $131.61 to $148.85 per share, which matched my prediction. My watch/defer recommendation did not change because the result was still below $337. The record also explains that I wrote the prediction on October 9, but the baseline commit was created at a later date. I did not backdate it. I just did not run it on that date.

Next, going over to my README-only cold-run record, in a fresh folder, it passed on my first attempt without having to use a third party. I used Codex for organization, code drafting, documentation, and review. I checked the final work against filings and used these validation results to support the output.
